A Reddit poster put the frustration plainly: with a small 4-to-5-cup drip coffee maker, the coffee starts to taste burnt after about a month — and it happens with every machine they buy. They tried washing the maker with vinegar. They tried changing brands. Nothing helped. "Why does my drip coffee taste burnt?" ([r/Coffee, 2022-12-08](https://www.reddit.com/r/Coffee/comments/zfnyza/why_does_my_drip_coffee_taste_burnt/))

The setup is a small drip maker in a small apartment, Lavazza coffee, and a tablespoon measure. The poster's own question was whether they should just buy a bigger machine. The replies say no: the size is not the problem. The schedule is the clue. When every machine turns burnt after about a month, something is building up inside the machine.

The question underneath is not really "which machine?" It is "why does the same cup keep turning burnt?" The answer, from the replies, is residue first — then the way the machine is filled, then the hot plate, then the beans.

## Burnt on a schedule means buildup

A burnt, ashy, smoky cup is different from a bitter one. Bitterness feels dry and harsh. Burnt tastes like charred toast or campfire smoke, and it coats the mouth. In this case the timing matters more than the taste: the first weeks are fine, and the burnt note arrives after about a month. That is the signature of residue, not of a bad roast or a wrong recipe. If the beans were the problem, the first cup would taste burnt too.

The most-supported reply (18 upvotes) suggests a test before buying anything: run the machine with plain water and no grounds, then taste the water. If the water itself tastes burnt, the flavor is coming from residue in the filter holder and the water path, not from the coffee. Coffee oil and fine grounds collect there, and after a month of daily brewing there is enough of it to flavor every cup.

## Vinegar is not the best cleaner for this

The poster had already tried vinegar, and it did not fix the taste. The top reply says vinegar is not the best tool for coffee gear, and suggests a cleaner made with sodium percarbonate instead — the kind sold for coffee equipment. Coffee residue is mostly oil, and a cleaner made for that job lifts it better than vinegar does.

Descaling is the other half of the same job. A reply suggests descaling once a month with a citric-acid powder, the kind sold as a coffee-machine descaler. Scale builds up inside the water path, the machine brews cooler and slower than it should, and the cup turns harsh. A monthly descale is a habit, not a repair: it keeps the buildup from reaching the point where the cup tastes burnt.

## Do not fill the machine with the coffee pot

The second most-supported reply (7 upvotes) points at a habit the poster had not noticed: filling the machine's water tank using the coffee pot itself. The pot holds coffee oil, and pouring water from it carries that oil into the machine, where it goes rancid. The poster replied that this made the most sense of all the advice they had received.

The fix costs nothing: fill the tank with a clean glass carafe or a jug that never holds coffee, not with the pot the coffee was brewed in. If the burnt taste started after about a month of the same filling habit, this is the most likely single cause.

## Get the coffee off the hot plate

A small drip maker usually keeps the pot on a hot plate, and the plate keeps cooking the coffee long after brewing. A reply adds a short instruction: wipe down the hot plate. Old coffee that has dried and burned onto the plate flavors the next pot from below.

The broader fix is the same one that applies to any drip maker: do not leave the coffee sitting on the heat. Pour out what will be drunk, and move the rest to a thermal carafe the moment brewing finishes. A cup that tastes fine right off the brew and turns burnt after twenty minutes on the plate has a hot-plate problem, not a bean problem.

## The beans come last, not first

With the machine clean and the filling habit fixed, the beans are the last thing to change. One reply put it plainly: the Lavazza the poster calls high-quality is a mid-range industrial coffee, not a specialty one, and trying a different bean is worth doing to see whether the burnt taste follows the beans or stays with the machine. A very dark roast also tastes burnt by design — sugars pushed past caramel into carbon — so a step back toward a medium roast is the direction to test.

Freshness still counts. Whole beans hold their aroma far longer than ground coffee, and a bag with no roast date that has sat open for weeks tastes flat whatever the recipe is. Keep the bag sealed between brews — our [coffee storage canisters](/en/articles/coffee-storage-canisters/) guide covers keeping air out — and grind just before brewing.

## What not to buy

- **A bigger coffee maker to fix the taste.** The poster asked whether a bigger machine would help. The replies say the size is not the cause — a bigger machine filled the same way and cleaned the same way turns burnt on the same schedule.
- **Another machine of the same kind before cleaning.** Changing brands did not help the poster, because the residue and the filling habit moved with them to the new machine.
- **A big bag of a bean you have not tried.** Test a different bean in a small bag first. If the burnt taste stays with the machine, the beans were never the problem.

## The question, answered

So why does the drip coffee taste burnt after about a month? Most likely the machine itself is the source: coffee oil and residue building up in the filter holder and the water path, helped along by filling the tank with the coffee pot. Run the machine with plain water and taste it — if the water tastes burnt, clean the machine with a cleaner made for coffee gear and descale it monthly with a citric-acid powder. Fill the tank with a clean jug, wipe the hot plate, and get the coffee off the heat once it is brewed. If the taste is still there after all of that, try a different bean, toward a medium roast.
