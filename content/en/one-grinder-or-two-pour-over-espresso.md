A poster on Home-Barista brews pour over in the morning and espresso in the afternoon, with one grinder doing both jobs. The grinder is a DF64 with brew burrs: one or two pour overs in the morning, then one or two espresso shots in the afternoon. The daily switch is the problem: "Will I be frustrated switching back and forth between the two settings?" ([Home-Barista, September 2023](https://www.home-barista.com/advice/two-grinders-filter-and-espresso-t89438-10.html))

The replies split into two camps, and both are right — for different routines. The answer is not which grinder, but how often you switch.

## Why the switch is frustrating

Espresso and pour over sit far apart on the grind dial. Espresso needs a fine grind with tiny steps; pour over needs a much coarser one. Every switch is a long move across the range, and back again.

Two things go wrong on the way back. The first is the setting itself: the poster found that after going to filter and back, the espresso number was off — "Sometimes I'd get dialed in at a number for espresso, change it to filter, put it back, and it would be off." The second is retention: grounds left inside the grinder mix into the next dose. "Sometimes I'd get fines in my filter, sometimes I'd notice some in my espresso."

So the frustration is real, and it is not about the grinder being bad at either method. It is about the round trip, twice a day.

## Making one grinder work

If the switch happens a few times a week rather than twice a day, one grinder can do the job — with a system, not memory. A switching guide puts it plainly: record two reference points, one for espresso and one for pour over, with a photograph and a written note. Memory alone is not reliable enough.

The moves themselves have an order. Going from espresso to pour over is the easy direction: run the chamber empty, move to the recorded coarse setting, and grind the filter dose. A small purge — grinding a few grams and discarding them — helps only when the flavor contrast is large. Coming back to espresso needs more care: clear the chamber first, and on many electric grinders run the motor while making a large move finer, so trapped fragments do not bind the burrs. Follow the grinder's manual here, and never force a collar that binds.

That workflow is the honest version of "one grinder for both." It costs a minute and a few grams each way, and it works — until the switching itself becomes the daily chore.

## When two grinders are the answer

If both methods are daily, the replies converge: give each method its own grinder and leave the settings alone. One reply put it as "set it and forget it" — not having to remember the last grind setting is the change that matters. Another keeps a dedicated espresso grinder and a second grinder for drip, so the espresso grinder never moves from its setting.

The second grinder does not need to be a second electric grinder. One reply's suggestion was direct: "Get a manual grinder for pourover (e.g. ZP6), keep the DF64 for espresso." A hand grinder covers pour over well, takes little counter space, and leaves the electric grinder on espresso duty.

The hand grinder I usually use is the Timemore Chestnut S3. I grind about 30g at a time, for 2–3 cups. Timemore's US official page lists pour over at 50–80 clicks as a starting point, on an external dial that moves 0.015mm per click. The full review, with its official specs, is in the [Chestnut S3 review](/en/articles/timemore-chestnut-s3/). I don't own an electric grinder, so the espresso side here is the thread's experience and the switching guides, not mine.

## What not to buy

- **A premium single grinder to fix the switching.** The poster's second option was to sell the DF64 and buy an end-game grinder. A better grinder still has to make the same round trip twice a day — the dial moves, the setting drifts, the retention stays. The upgrade fixes the grind, not the switching.
- **A second grinder for the method you rarely brew.** If espresso is a weekend thing and pour over is the daily cup, one grinder and the recorded-settings workflow is enough. Buy the second grinder for the method you brew daily, not the one you brew sometimes.

## The question, answered

So: one grinder or two? If you switch a few times a week, keep one grinder and write down both settings — photograph the dial, note the numbers, purge a few grams on the way back to espresso. If you brew pour over in the morning and espresso in the afternoon, get a second grinder, and a hand grinder for pour over is the cheaper way to do it. Either way, the fix is in the routine, not in a more expensive single grinder.
