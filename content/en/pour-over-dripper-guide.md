A pour-over dripper is a funnel that holds the grounds while hot water passes through them. The shape of the funnel and the number of holes in it set how fast the water drains, and that changes how the coffee tastes. This guide covers the three shapes: cone, trapezoid, and flat-bottom wave.

**Quick pick:** want to steer the cup with your pour — a cone, the Hario V60. Want the same cup every time without practising a pour — a trapezoid or a flat-bottom wave, the Kalita Wave.

This is a review summary. We have not used any dripper. The facts below are from the makers' pages and published guides, as recorded in this site's Japanese dripper comparison (pages opened October 30, 2026), and links go to Amazon.com search results, so compare sellers and check the price and stock on the day you buy.

## The three shapes at a glance

|  | Cone | Trapezoid | Flat-bottom wave |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Hario V60, Kono | Kalita, Melitta | Kalita Wave |
| Drain holes | One large hole | One to three holes | Three small holes |
| Drain | Fast; the pour sets the pace | Slower; the taste stays steady | Water pools on a flat bed; the pour matters less |
| Cup | Bright and adjustable by how you pour | Even and repeatable | Even, with less variation between brews |

A cone suits a drinker who wants to chase a brighter cup by changing the pour. A trapezoid or a wave suits a drinker who wants the same cup every time with a steadier hand.

## 1. Cone — the Hario V60

The cone is a 60-degree funnel with a single large hole at the bottom and spiral ribs on the inside wall. Water drains fast, so the speed and pattern of the pour set how long the water stays with the grounds: pour fast for a lighter cup, pour slow for a richer one. The V60 comes in plastic, ceramic, glass, and metal, in sizes 01, 02, and 03 — a 02 covers 1–4 cups.

The cone is the pick with the highest skill ceiling: it rewards a steady, slow pour and punishes a rushed one. It also needs its own cone paper filters, and the size must match the dripper — a 02 dripper takes 02 filters.

## 2. Trapezoid — Kalita and Melitta

The trapezoid is a wedge with a flat bottom and one to three small holes. The water drains slower than in a cone, so the brew is less sensitive to how the water is poured and the taste comes out steadier from one morning to the next. Kalita's trapezoid drippers take Kalita trapezoid filters; Melitta's take Melitta #2 cone filters, which are sold in grocery stores.

The Melitta 1-cup pour-over is a light plastic cone for one mug — a travel or office pick that weighs almost nothing. It is the lowest-stakes way into pour-over: the dripper is cheap, the filters are everywhere, and a single small hole keeps the drawdown slow without any technique.

## 3. Flat-bottom wave — the Kalita Wave

The wave is a flat-bottom dripper: the grounds spread across a wide, even bed, and the water drains through three small holes. The flat bed and the three holes keep the flow steady even when the pour is uneven, so the cup comes out full-bodied without a practised hand. That is why published tests call it the most forgiving dripper of the three shapes.

The trade-off is the filter. The Wave takes its own wave-shaped paper filters, which cost more than V60 or Melitta filters and are less widely stocked — usually an online buy rather than a grocery-store one. Sizes are 155 for 1–2 cups and 185 for 1–4 cups. Ceramic and glass versions have ribs on the bottom that stop the wet filter from suctioning against the drain holes; the stainless version is known for that suction problem in published reviews.

## The material matters too

- **Plastic:** light, hard to break, and cheap. The right first dripper.
- **Ceramic and porcelain:** hold heat well and look good, but break and weigh more.
- **Glass:** takes on no smell, and shows the brew.
- **Metal (stainless steel, copper):** tough, with good heat conduction.

Plastic holds brew temperature better than ceramic because it draws less heat out of the water. Ceramic needs a hot-water rinse first to warm up, or the first pour loses heat to the dripper.

## Match the paper filter to the shape

A dripper and its paper filter must be the same shape: cone with cone, trapezoid with trapezoid, wave with wave-only paper. A mismatched filter floats, or drains differently from what the shape was made for.

Rinse the paper with hot water before brewing — it takes the paper smell out. And mind the size: a 2–4 cup dripper brewing a single cup leaves a thin bed of grounds, and the coffee comes out weak. Brew the size the dripper was made for.

## How to pick one

**How much control do you want?** If the pour itself is the fun, the cone gives you the most to steer. If you want the dripper to do the steering, the trapezoid or the wave keeps the flow steady for you.

**How many cups?** A 02 cone or a 185 wave covers 1–4 cups; a 155 wave or a Melitta 1-cup is for one mug. A dripper made for 2–4 cups brews a thin cup when it makes only one.

**Can you buy the filters?** V60 and Melitta #2 filters are easy to find. Wave filters are a proprietary shape — check that you can buy them where you live before choosing the Wave.

**Will it fit what you brew into?** Check that the dripper sits on your server or mug. The dripper's base diameter and the "for 1–2 cups" label are what to match, not the brand alone.

**What grind?** Pour-over takes a medium-fine to medium grind — finer tends to taste bitter, coarser tends to taste thin.
