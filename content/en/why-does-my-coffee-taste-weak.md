A Reddit poster put the frustration plainly: pour over in a large mug, two scoops of coffee, water just off the boil — and the cup still tastes thin and flavorless. "Weak coffee with pourover, any tips?" ([r/Coffee, 2014-02-23](https://www.reddit.com/r/Coffee/comments/1yms2e/weak_coffee_with_pourover_any_tips/))

The setup is two scoops of beans in a blade grinder, ground about as fine as store-bought ground coffee, then hot water poured from an electric kettle just above the grounds and stirred. The replies agreed on the diagnosis before they agreed on anything else: the cup is weak because there is not enough coffee in it for that much water. The fix starts with the dose, not the beans.

## Weak usually means too little coffee for the water

A weak, watery cup is first of all a strength problem: too little coffee dissolved into too much water. Two scoops is roughly 14–16 g. For a large mug — 12 to 14 ounces, about 350–400 ml — the most-supported reply put it plainly: that dose is short, and the mug needs at least 22–24 g. Another reply gave the same rule as a ratio: about 25 g for 12 ounces.

The usual starting point is about 60 g of coffee per litre of water, a 1:15 to 1:16 ratio. That means roughly 20 g for a 300 ml cup, and 24 g for a 400 ml mug. The poster's two scoops land near 1:25 — more water per gram than the cup can carry flavor through. Add another scoop first, weigh it if you can, and taste the next cup before changing anything else.

A scale takes the guessing out of the dose. Scoops vary with the bean and how heaped they are, so the same "two scoops" can be 14 g one morning and 18 g the next. Weighing the coffee and the water makes the fix repeatable.

## Then check the grind and the pour

If the stronger dose helps but the cup is still thin, the replies' next steps are the grind and the pour.

**Grind finer, one step at a time.** Grounds that are too coarse let the water rush through, and the flavor stays in the grounds. Move one step finer, taste the next cup, and only then decide whether to move again. A blade grinder makes this harder: it chops unevenly, so some grounds are fine dust and some are coarse chunks, and the cup comes out muddled either way. A burr grinder gives one even size, which is the whole point of grinding at home.

**Bloom, then pour slowly.** The replies suggested a 30–50 second bloom — wet all the grounds, wait while the gas escapes — then pour in slow, steady circles instead of dumping the water in. A fast pour pushes water through the bed before it has time to pull flavor out. A gooseneck kettle makes the slow pour easy to control, which is why several replies recommended one.

**Keep the water hot enough.** Water below about 90 °C (195 °F) cannot extract enough flavor, no matter how fine the grind. Water just off the boil, around 90–96 °C, is the usual range. If the kettle has sat for a few minutes, reheat it.

## Weak is not the same as sour

It helps to tell the two apart, because the fixes point in opposite directions. A sour cup is sharp and vinegary — under-extracted, the water stopped before the sweetness came out. A weak cup is thin and diluted, like coffee-flavored water, with little flavor of any kind. The poster's cup is weak: the flavor is missing, not sharp. That is why the dose comes first. Grinding finer helps a sour cup; for a weak cup, more coffee per cup is the bigger lever.

Freshness still counts. Whole beans hold their aroma far longer than ground coffee, and a bag with no roast date that has sat open for weeks tastes flat whatever the recipe is. Keep the bag sealed between brews — our [coffee storage canisters](/en/articles/coffee-storage-canisters/) guide covers keeping air out — and grind just before brewing.

## What not to buy

- **A new grinder to fix a weak cup.** The poster already owns a grinder. A weak cup from too little coffee is a recipe problem, not a grinder problem — add a scoop before buying anything.
- **Darker beans to "cover" the thinness.** A darker roast adds its own bitter edge. It hides nothing.
- **A big bag of a bean you have not tried.** Buy the size you finish while it is fresh, and change one thing at a time so the fix is visible.

## The question, answered

So why does the pour over taste weak? Most likely the dose: two scoops, about 14–16 g, in a large mug is too little coffee for that much water. Raise the dose to about 22–24 g for a 12–14 ounce mug — roughly 60 g per litre — bloom for 30–50 seconds, pour slowly, and keep the water at 90–96 °C. The beans are the last thing to change.
