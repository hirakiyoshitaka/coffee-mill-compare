An alcohol lamp is lit. Inside the glass, water rises without a sound. When the flame goes out, amber coffee falls quietly back down. A siphon brews coffee with flame and glass alone.

Only a handful of companies still make one. Three facts first (sources are at the end of this page):

- The balance-style siphon built by a Belgian company ended when that company closed. Cheaper mass-produced copies now circulate under the "Belgian style" name.
- As far as we could find, only a very small number of workshops still hand-build a fine balance-style siphon.
- The classic vacuum-style Cona keeps the shape designed in 1962, still made by a factory in the Netherlands.

## How a siphon works

Water is heated in the lower flask, and the steam pressure it makes pushes hot water up into the upper funnel. The grounds meet the water there, and when the heat stops, the lower flask cools, the pressure falls, and the coffee passes through the filter back down.

Pouring skill matters less than with a hand drip, because the taste is set by the steeping time and the stirring. Follow the same steps each time, and the cup repeats more easily.

## Cona: the all-glass classic

[Cona's official site](https://www.cona.be)

Designed in 1962 by Abram Games, an all-glass vacuum brewer. The maker's page says water and coffee touch only glass, with no paper or cloth filter — a glass filter rod leaves no aftertaste. It holds 1.1 liters, about 8 cups, and with preheated water it reaches the table in about 6 minutes. The fuel is a bioethanol-type alcohol. Every part is replaceable.

The maker today is CONA Factory (A. Gezang & Co BV) in The Hague, Netherlands. The official site does not state shipping to Japan, and we have not confirmed it.

The shape many people picture when they hear "siphon": a small alcohol flame behind glass. Hario, the Japanese glass maker, has built this standard for years, and it is often the first name mentioned for starting with a siphon.

## The brewing steps (from the makers' instructions)

Hario's instructions, in order only (checked November 14, 2026; sources at the end of this page).

1. Boil water in a kettle first, to save time. Fit the filter to the upper funnel.
2. Put the grounds in the funnel. The maker's guide gives about 10–12g per cup, medium-fine, as a starting point.
3. Pour about 120 mL of hot water per cup into the lower flask. Wipe the outside of the flask dry.
4. Light the alcohol lamp (or switch the electric model on) and set it under the flask.
5. When the water has been pushed up, gently sink the grounds with a bamboo paddle and let them mix in.
6. Leave it for about 40–60 seconds, then stop the heat (some guides say about 60 seconds after the grounds sink).
7. When the coffee has fallen down completely, lift the upper funnel off and pour from the lower flask.

The amount of beans and water differs slightly between guides. Once you buy one, follow the manual that comes with it.

## What people who used one say (with sources)

Not our voice. Summaries of published articles, with the source and date on each heading.

**Kakakumag (December 25, 2019). The writer bought a Hario Technica 3-cup and recorded a first brew.**
- Good: it worked the first time by following the manual. Compared with the same beans through a drip, it had no harsh edge, a mellow mouthfeel, and a clean finish. The brewer felt like an experiment, and watching the water rise and fall was fun.
- Trouble: setting the brewer up is a bother. Caring for the cloth filter and the alcohol lamp, and tidying up, are a bother too. Shorter extraction turned out thin; the writer's own preference was about 45 seconds. Grinding too fine made the taste uneven.
- The writer's conclusion was that on a slow holiday morning, the care is hardly a problem.

**CoffeeGeek (Mark Prince, July 21, 2020, updated June 15, 2023). A guide written from using a balance brewer.**
- Good: the writer calls the Belgian-designed balance Cafetino "one of the most eye-catching beautiful coffee devices" he has seen or used (summary). Brewing takes about 5 minutes.
- Trouble: starting from cold water can take 15 minutes or more to heat over an alcohol flame. That Cafetino stopped being made about 20 years ago and rarely appears even second-hand.

**Acquired Coffee (Rosie Greaves, updated June 2023). A comparison of balance siphons.**
- Good: brewing with a balance siphon is called "one of the most dramatic ways to brew," and with practice it becomes a meditative, calming time (summary).
- Trouble: it also says the brewer asks for more time and money than a drip or a French press.

**The Coffee Bean Laboratory (コーヒー豆研究所) lists these shared troubles:** water not rising (the funnel tube is not under the water in the flask, so pressure escapes), refilling the alcohol, and wind.

In short, the praise is "fun to watch," "a clean taste," and "a richer slow morning." The complaints are "the setup and care take effort" and "the taste is uneven until you get used to it." The effort is real. This is a brewer for people who enjoy the effort itself.

## The balance siphon, another shape

Besides the vacuum type — flask and funnel stacked vertically — there is a shape called the balance (balancing) siphon. Two vessels sit left and right, joined by a seesaw-like arm.

It works like this. Water heated in the left glass ball moves through a tube to the right pot. When the right side grows heavy, the balance tips and a lid closes over the alcohol lamp by itself. With the flame out, the pressure falls and the coffee returns. Nobody has to put the flame out; the brewer finishes itself.

A shape once used by 19th-century royalty is still made as a revival today. Hand-built pieces can carry very high prices (source at the end of this page). Few makers build them, and they are not easy to buy in Japan. This is only "another shape exists" — the KONO and Hario sections above are the siphons to start with at home.

## Why a fine siphon lasts

No prices here. Facts only.

- **Parts can be replaced, so it can last.** Cona's official page says all parts are replaceable. Hario says the Technica's glass balls can be replaced part by part. A broken glass does not end it; it is built to be repaired and kept.
- **A coffee-shop brewer, at home.** KONO's maker, Coffee Syphon Co., says on its official online shop that its founder invented and put the coffee siphon to practical use in 1925. The same line of brewer you see on an old coffee-shop counter comes in a home size.
- **It is fun just to watch.** The water boils below, rises, swims with the grounds, and falls back down — all of it visible through the glass. The published reviews mention this watching time too.
- **The flame itself is part of the view.** A lamp flame gives a sight an electric switch cannot.

## When it does not go well: causes and fixes

Only what the official guides and the reviewers' articles state. Nothing without a source is written here.

**The water does not rise to the upper funnel**
- Hario's FAQ lists four causes: the upper ball is set into the lower ball at an angle / the siphon rubber is damaged and air leaks / the cloth filter's mesh is clogged with grounds / parts do not fit after a replacement.
- Fixes: set the funnel straight again. Replace the rubber. Wash the cloth with a toothbrush or similar, and replace it if that does not help (about 60 uses is the guide).
- Source: [Hario FAQ](https://www.hario.com/faq/)

**A little water stays in the lower flask**
- Not a fault. Hario explains that a small amount is left on purpose, to prevent the flask from boiling dry.
- Source: [Hario FAQ](https://www.hario.com/faq/)

**Grounds fall into the lower flask**
- Hario's guide lists three causes: the filter hook is not caught on the tip of the upper ball's leg tube, the filter sits off-center, or the grounds are too fine.
- Fixes: hook the filter back on slowly and carefully. Grind a little coarser (medium-fine or coarser is recommended to keep grounds out).
- Source: [Hario FAQ (siphon)](https://www.hario.com/product/syphon.pdf)

**The grounds float and do not sink**
- KONO's steps say that once the water has risen and the surface settles, tap the floating grounds down into the water with the flat of a bamboo paddle, then loosen them so they do not clump.
- Source: [KONO brewing guide](https://coffee-syphon.co.jp/manual/)

**Harshness or a heavy aftertaste**
- KONO explains that stirring firmly at the start splits the brew into three layers — liquid, grounds, and foam — which separates the harsh components. Stirring after the flame is out, just before the liquid falls, mixes those harsh components back into the coffee.
- Fix: finish stirring at the start, and do not stir after the flame is out.
- Source: [KONO: coffee siphon](https://coffee-syphon.co.jp/coffee_syphon/)

**Thin taste**
- The Kakakumag writer found that a shorter extraction (heating) time made a thinner cup, and about 45 seconds was the writer's own preference.
- Fix: stay inside the official time range first (Hario 40–60 seconds, KONO 40–60 seconds by size), then move it a little at a time to find your own preference.
- Source: [Kakakumag](https://kakakumag.com/houseware/?id=14836)

For "bitter," the Hario and KONO official pages do not state a cause and fix directly, so this page does not decide one.

## Fire and glass: care

A siphon uses fire and glass. Hario's stated cautions, as written:

- Pour hot water slowly.
- Before putting the flask over the flame, wipe the outside dry.
- Do not fill the alcohol lamp to the top.
- Be careful when putting the flame out.
- Glass and parts are hot afterward — use gloves or mitts.
- Do not push the upper funnel into its stand.
- Wind bends a lamp flame and makes the heat reach poorly. Keep wind out.
- When the flame goes out, the coffee falls down, so follow the steps for the timing.

A glass ball can break. For the Technica, the maker says the balls can be replaced part by part.

## Which one fits?

- **An all-glass design classic:** Cona
- **A wooden stand from a maker since 1925:** KONO
- **The coffee-shop sight, starting at home today:** Hario Technica
- **A home where flame is not an option:** Hario electric siphon

A siphon is not a brewer for speed. It is a brewer for people who can turn the few minutes of waiting for the water to rise into minutes of watching. If you can picture that time on a slow morning, watching the small view inside the glass while one cup waits, one of the four above is likely yours.

## FAQ

**Does fuel come with the alcohol lamp?**
Hario's Technica page says alcohol is not included. Buy fuel alcohol separately.

**Can a beginner brew with it?**
The steps are the seven above. The Kakakumag writer succeeded on the first try. The writer also says the extraction time and the grind took a few tries to fit to taste.

**What if flame worries me?**
There is an electric model. The Hario ECA-3-B is electric, with dial-controlled heat.

**What if the glass breaks?**
Both Cona and the Hario Technica state on their official pages that glass parts can be replaced part by part.

**What grind do I use?**
Hario's guide gives medium-fine as the starting point.
