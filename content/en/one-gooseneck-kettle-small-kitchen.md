A Reddit poster put the hesitation plainly: they own a regular electric kettle and want to start pour-over, but the kitchen is small and keeping two kettles feels like too much — "Limited kitchen space - gooseneck kettle?" ([r/Coffee, about six years ago](https://www.reddit.com/r/Coffee/comments/i666rt/limited_kitchen_space_gooseneck_kettle/))

The question underneath is not really "which kettle?" It is "do I have to add a second kettle to a kitchen that has no room for one?" The answer is no — and the fix is to replace, not to add.

## Why two kettles feels like too much

The regular kettle already does its job. A second kettle is a single-purpose tool taking counter space, and pour-over is the only brew method that asks for it. A regular kettle pours fast and wide: tip it and the water comes out in a gush, flooding one side of the coffee bed and leaving dry grounds on the other. A gooseneck spout narrows the stream to a thin, steady flow you can aim — a slow pour to wet all the grounds first, then steady circles to keep the bed even. That control is what makes one cup taste like the next.

So the kettle question is real. The space question has a simpler answer than buying a second kettle.

## One kettle does both jobs

A gooseneck kettle can do almost everything a regular kettle can: boil water for tea, fill a mug, fill a teapot. The top reply in the thread makes exactly this point — sell or give away the regular kettle and let the gooseneck be the only kettle. One kettle on the counter, doing both jobs.

That only works if the gooseneck fits the rest of the kitchen. Two things to check before swapping:

- **Capacity.** A gooseneck holds less than a family kettle — our [gooseneck kettle guide](/en/articles/gooseneck-kettle-guide/) puts 0.6–0.8 L as the guide for 1–2 cups, and around 1 L for a family. If you boil a litre at a time for a household, a small gooseneck means boiling twice.
- **Heat source.** An electric gooseneck plugs in and boils like the kettle it replaces. A stovetop gooseneck needs a hob — gas, or IH-compatible if that is what you have.

If both fit, the swap is the whole answer: one kettle, pour-over included, nothing added to the counter.

## If you keep the regular kettle: the small pourer

If the regular kettle stays — for a household, for tea, for everything else — the gap can be bridged with something that lives in a cupboard instead of on the counter. Boil in the regular kettle, then pour the hot water into a small gooseneck pourer and brew from that.

One reply in the thread points to the smallest option they had seen: the Hario Air, a plastic gooseneck pourer that holds about 350 ml. Another reply used a small tin pitcher, about the size of a milk-frothing pitcher, warmed first with hot water. Both are the same idea: the pouring vessel does not have to be the kettle that boils the water. We have not used either one, so treat them as leads from the thread, not as our picks — the point is the size, not the model.

A pourer this small brews one cup at a time. For a single morning cup, that is the whole job.

## Or skip the pour: immersion needs no gooseneck

There is a third answer, and it needs no new kettle at all. A gooseneck matters only when your hand delivers the water to the grounds — pour-over, in other words. Immersion brewers do not care how the water gets in: the grounds sit in hot water for a few minutes either way. If the goal is better coffee at home rather than pour-over specifically, a French press or an AeroPress brews well with the regular kettle you already own. The [French press vs AeroPress guide](/en/articles/french-press-vs-aeropress/) covers the brew itself: about 4 minutes of steeping, water at 92–96 °C, and a coarse grind.

Pour-over is the method that asks for the kettle. If pour-over is the goal, the kettle question comes back. If the goal is a better cup with no new hardware, immersion answers it today.

## Before the kettle: the grind

One honest note before any kettle money changes hands. A gooseneck makes the pour repeatable; it does not fix stale beans or an uneven grind. Grind quality changes the cup more than a kettle does, and fresh grounds matter most of all. The hand grinder I usually use is the Timemore Chestnut S3. I grind about 30g at a time, for 2–3 cups. The full review, with its official specs, is in the [Chestnut S3 review](/en/articles/timemore-chestnut-s3/), and the [coffee grind size guide](/en/articles/coffee-grind-size-guide/) shows where each brew method sits. If the beans are still pre-ground from a bag, a grinder earns its place before a kettle does.

## What you do not need yet

The rest of the set can wait until a specific annoyance shows up:

- **A second kettle:** the point of this article. Replace the regular kettle, or bridge it with a small pourer — do not add a second full-size kettle to a small kitchen.
- **Variable temperature:** a separate decision from the spout. Boiling and resting the water works to start; an electric kettle that sets and holds a temperature is a convenience for later. The [gooseneck kettle guide](/en/articles/gooseneck-kettle-guide/) covers the choice.
- **A new dripper:** the V60 you have is fine. A flat-bottomed dripper forgives a rough pour more than a cone does, but it is not a fix for a kettle problem — the pourer or the swap is.

## The one honest warning

A gooseneck does not make coffee taste better by itself. What it changes is the error rate: the same pour, the same way, cup after cup. And it holds less water than the kettle it replaces — for a big household pot, expect to boil twice. If the kitchen truly has room for one kettle and one kettle only, make it the gooseneck, and let the regular kettle go.

## The question, answered

So do you need a second kettle for pour-over in a small kitchen? No. One gooseneck replaces the regular kettle and does both jobs; a small pourer bridges the gap if the regular kettle stays; and an immersion brewer needs no special kettle at all. Buy one thing — or nothing — and keep the counter as it is.
