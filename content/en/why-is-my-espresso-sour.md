A Reddit poster put the frustration plainly: with a DeLonghi Dedica and a Sage Smart Grinder, the shot takes about 30 seconds, the crema looks good, and the coffee still tastes like straight acid — "My Espresso comes out too sour even when all characteristics are good" ([r/Coffee, 2022-12-18](https://www.reddit.com/r/Coffee/comments/zobt5g/my_espresso_comes_out_too_sour_even_when_all/))

The setup is 15 g of coffee to about 30 ml out, in about 30 seconds, with fresh beans from good specialty roasters — several different ones, all with the same result. The poster even tried a bottomless portafilter, then went back to the pressurized basket because it is more forgiving for a beginner. The crema looks good on both, then mostly disappears after a minute.

The question underneath is not really "which beans?" It is "why does a shot with the right numbers still taste sour?" The answer, from the replies, is that the numbers are only half the story — and the fix most people agreed on is a longer shot.

## Sour usually means too little came out

A sour, sharp, thin shot is usually under-extracted: the water stopped before the sweetness came out. The compounds dissolve in a rough order — sour first, then sweet, then bitter — so a shot that only got through the first stage tastes like acid and nothing else.

That is why a shot can hit 30 seconds and still be sour. The clock says the water spent enough time in the puck, but if the water found a fast path through, or the dose was too small for the yield, the sweetness never arrived.

## Pull a longer shot first

The most-supported reply in the thread, with 44 upvotes, said to increase the output: try a 1:2.5 or 1:3 ratio. The bitter compounds come out at the end, so running a longer shot can balance the cup out. Other replies said the same thing in their own numbers — 15 g in to 45 g out, or shots of 45–60 g.

For this poster that means keeping the 15 g dose and letting the shot run to about 40–45 g out, instead of stopping at 30 ml. Weigh the output — a scale takes the guessing out of the yield — and keep everything else the same for the next shot.

## Then grind finer, but watch the machine

The next most common advice was to grind finer, one small step at a time, until the shot chokes and then back off a little. A finer grind slows the water and raises extraction, which is exactly what a sour shot needs.

There is a limit here, and the poster already hit it: going any finer, the machine starts struggling to push the espresso out. A Dedica is a small thermoblock machine, and a very fine grind can choke it. If finer does not work, the longer ratio above is the fix to reach for instead — it adds extraction without asking the machine for more pressure.

## Check the temperature

The poster's own guess was the water: it might not be hot enough on the Dedica. Several replies agreed. The Dedica has three temperature settings, and the highest one is the place to start for a sour shot. Cool water extracts less and favours the sharp, acidic compounds.

A thermoblock machine also needs its group head warm. The water can be hot enough while the group head itself is still at room temperature, which cools the shot on the way through. Run a blank shot of water through the empty group before pulling the real one, and let the machine finish its full heat-up time.

## The basket and the puck

The poster went back to the pressurized basket because it is more forgiving. Several replies suggested the opposite direction: try the normal, non-pressurized basket, which gives a truer read on the grind and the puck. A pressurized basket can hide a grind problem by forcing crema through a small valve, while the coffee underneath stays under-extracted.

On tamping, the replies split. One said to pack the shot tightly; another said the harder the tamp, the more the flow is restricted and the more sour the shot gets. The point both sides share is that the puck should be even and level, so the water does not channel through one weak spot. A distribution tool that breaks up clumps before tamping is the cheap way to get there.

If the shot tastes sour and bitter at the same time, that is the signature of channeling: water over-extracts the weak spots while the rest of the puck stays under-extracted. Fix the puck prep before touching the grinder.

## The beans come last, not first

With the ratio, the grind, and the temperature checked, the beans are the last thing to change — and the replies' suggestion was a direction, not a brand: a darker roast. Light roasts are naturally more acidic and harder to extract, so they tip toward sour more easily, and in espresso that acidity shows up stronger than in a pour-over. A darker roast extracts more readily and sits further from the sour edge.

Freshness still counts. Whole beans hold their aroma far longer than ground coffee, and a bag with no roast date that has sat open for weeks tastes flat whatever the recipe is. Keep the bag sealed between brews — our [coffee storage canisters](/en/articles/coffee-storage-canisters/) guide covers keeping air out — and grind just before brewing.

The hand grinder I usually use is the Timemore Chestnut S3. I grind about 30g at a time, for 2–3 cups. The full review, with its official specs, is in the [Chestnut S3 review](/en/articles/timemore-chestnut-s3/).

## What not to buy

- **A new grinder to fix a sour shot.** The poster already owns a good burr grinder. A sour shot from a burr grinder is a recipe problem, not a grinder problem.
- **A finer grind at any cost.** If the machine chokes, finer is not the answer. Pull longer instead.
- **A big bag of a bean you have not tried.** Buy the size you finish while it is fresh, and change one thing at a time so the fix is visible.

## The question, answered

So why is the espresso sour when the time and the crema look right? Most likely the shot is too short for the dose: 15 g to 30 ml stops right where the acids live. Pull it longer, to about 1:2.5 or 1:3, and let the sweetness catch up. If the cup is still sour, grind a step finer — unless the machine chokes — and check that the temperature is on the highest setting and the group head is warm. The beans are the last thing to change, toward a darker roast.
