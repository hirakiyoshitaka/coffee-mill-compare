A Reddit poster put the frustration plainly: with a Moccamaster drip machine, a Fellow Ode 2 grinder, and a scale, the same procedure gives a bitter cup four times out of five — "Why my coffee is 4/5 times bitter and over all bad?" ([r/Coffee, 2026-07-05](https://www.reddit.com/r/Coffee/comments/1unyvuq/why_my_coffee_is_45_times_bitter_and_over_all_bad/))

The setup is 15 g of coffee to about 300 ml of water, at grind setting 7–9, and about 30 different beans have all come out bitter. That last detail is the clue. When thirty beans all taste bitter, the beans are probably not the problem. The recipe is.

The question underneath is not really "which beans?" It is "why does the same cup keep coming out bitter?" The answer, from the replies, is the ratio first — then the grind, then the water, then the beans.

## Bitter usually means too much came out

A bitter, harsh, drying cup is usually over-extracted: the water pulled too much out of the grounds. The opposite cup, sour and thin, is usually under-extracted: the water stopped before the sweetness came out. So a bitter cup wants less extraction, not more.

The ratio decides how much each gram of coffee has to give up. At 15 g to 300 ml of water — a 1:20 ratio — each gram is worked hard, and the water keeps pulling until the bitter compounds come out too. The replies suggested 18–20 g per 300 g of water, about 60 g per litre. More coffee for the same water means each gram gives up less, and the bitter edge drops away.

That is the first thing to change, because it is the biggest lever here and the easiest to measure. Weigh the coffee and the water — a scale takes the guessing out of the dose — and keep everything else the same for the next cup.

## Then grind coarser

If the stronger ratio helps but the cup is still bitter, the replies' next step is a coarser grind. Coarser grounds give the water less surface to work on, so it pulls out less. Move one step coarser, taste the next cup, and only then decide whether to move again.

The [coffee grind size guide](/en/articles/coffee-grind-size-guide/) shows where each brew method sits, so the starting point is not a guess. And change one thing at a time: if the dose and the grind move together, there is no way to know which one fixed the cup.

## What the machine and the water do

A drip machine sets its own water temperature, so temperature is not a lever the poster can pull the way a pour-over brewer can. What is still worth checking is the water itself: very hard water or stale water in the tank can make a cup taste harsh no matter what the dose is. Fresh, cold water in the tank and a clean machine are the cheap checks before buying anything.

A clean machine matters more than it sounds. Old coffee oil left in the brewer turns later cups bitter, and a drip machine that has never been descaled brews cooler and slower than it should. If the bitterness came on gradually while nothing else changed, cleaning is the first suspect, not the beans.

## The beans come last, not first

With the ratio and the grind fixed, the beans are the last thing to change — and the replies' suggestion was a direction, not a brand: a medium roast from Brazil or Colombia for a soft, nutty cup. Light roasts and very dark roasts both push toward the edges, bright and sharp on one side, roasty and bitter on the other. A medium roast sits in the middle, which is where a brewer who keeps getting bitter cups wants to be.

Freshness still counts. Whole beans hold their aroma far longer than ground coffee, and a bag with no roast date that has sat open for weeks tastes flat whatever the recipe is. Keep the bag sealed between brews — our [coffee storage canisters](/en/articles/coffee-storage-canisters/) guide covers keeping air out — and grind just before brewing.

The hand grinder I usually use is the Timemore Chestnut S3. I grind about 30g at a time, for 2–3 cups. The full review, with its official specs, is in the [Chestnut S3 review](/en/articles/timemore-chestnut-s3/).

## What not to buy

- **A new grinder to fix a bitter cup.** The poster already owns a good burr grinder. A bitter cup from a burr grinder is a recipe problem, not a grinder problem.
- **Darker beans to "cover" the bitterness.** A darker roast adds its own bitter edge. It hides nothing.
- **A big bag of a bean you have not tried.** Buy the size you finish while it is fresh, and change one thing at a time so the fix is visible.

## The question, answered

So why is the coffee bitter four times out of five? Most likely the ratio: 15 g to 300 ml is thin enough that each gram is over-extracted. Raise the dose to 18–20 g per 300 g of water, grind a step coarser if the cup is still bitter, and keep the water fresh and the machine clean. The beans are the last thing to change — and after thirty bitter bags, the recipe, not the bag, is where the fix lives.
