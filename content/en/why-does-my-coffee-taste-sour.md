A poster on Coffee Forums asked for help with a pour over that kept coming out sour: "Need help with pour over! Cant solve sour coffee problem." ([Coffee Forums, 2023-12-20](https://www.coffeeforums.com/threads/need-help-with-pour-over-cant-solve-sour-coffee-problem.23495/))

The replies agreed on the diagnosis before they agreed on anything else: a sour cup is usually under-extracted. The water stopped pulling flavor out of the grounds too soon, so the acids came out and the sweetness that should balance them never arrived. It is a brewing problem, not a fault in the beans — and it is fixable one change at a time.

## Sour means too little came out

When hot water meets ground coffee, the flavors do not all come out at once. The bright, acidic notes dissolve first; the sugars and the body that round the cup out come later; the bitter compounds come last. Stop the brew early and the cup holds mostly the first part — sharp, thin, and unfinished.

That is why a sour cup and a bitter cup point in opposite directions. Sour means the water took too little; bitter means it took too much. The Specialty Coffee Association's target for filter coffee is an extraction of 18 to 22 percent of the coffee's weight — below that, a cup tends to taste sour and thin.

Sourness is also not the same as acidity. A good light roast is bright on purpose, and that brightness arrives with sweetness and body. Sourness is brightness arriving alone, with nothing behind it — like unripe fruit instead of ripe fruit.

## Grind finer first

Grind is the biggest lever, and the replies' first move. Finer grounds give the water more surface to work on, so it pulls out more — and a finer grind also slows the water down, which adds contact time on its own.

Move one step finer, brew the next cup, and taste before moving again. The thread's advice was to go one click at a time and keep going until the sourness lifts — rarely more than a few clicks. Changing the grind and the water temperature together hides which one fixed the cup, so change one thing per brew.

The [coffee grind size guide](/en/articles/coffee-grind-size-guide/) shows where each brew method sits, so the starting point is not a guess.

## Then hotter water and more time

If the cup is still sour after the grind moves finer, the next two levers are water temperature and brew time. Water at 90 to 96 °C (195 to 205 °F) extracts fully; water that has sat cooling in the kettle, or a brewer that never gets hot enough, leaves the cup under-extracted. Without a thermometer, bring the kettle to a full boil and let it rest off the heat for 30 to 45 seconds.

Brew time is the third lever. A pour over that drains in well under two and a half minutes raced through before the job was done — a finer grind usually slows it down on its own. For a light roast, which is denser and harder to extract, the replies suggested about three minutes of brew time and water near the top of the range.

## Check the ratio — but do not just add coffee

A watery, sour cup can also be a ratio problem. Weigh the dose: a starting point of about 1 gram of coffee to 15 grams of water — 18 to 20 g per 300 g of water — keeps the cup from going thin. A [coffee scale](/en/articles/coffee-scale-guide/) takes the guessing out of it.

One trap is worth knowing: adding more coffee does not fix a sour cup on its own. A strong cup that is still under-extracted is still sour — a UC Davis study found sourness highest in brews that were both strong and under-extracted. Fix the extraction first, then adjust the strength.

## When sour and bitter arrive together

If the cup tastes sour and bitter at the same time, the problem is usually uneven extraction: some of the grounds over-extracted while the rest barely brewed. Water channeling through one spot in the bed, or a grinder that makes a mix of fine dust and coarse chunks, does this. The fix is the pour and the grind, not the ratio: wet all the grounds evenly during the bloom, pour gently in circles instead of drilling into one spot, and keep the bed level. ## The beans and the water come last

If the recipe is right and the cup is still sour, check the beans. Stale beans taste flat and sharp no matter what the recipe is — look for a roast date, and buy amounts you finish in a week or two. A very light roast can also simply be brighter than the drinker's taste; if it stays sour after dialing in, a medium roast is the next thing to try, not a new grinder.

Water matters too: very low-mineral water — distilled or reverse-osmosis water on its own — has little in it to pull flavor out, and can leave a cup sour and thin. Filtered tap water keeps the minerals the cup needs.

## What not to buy

- **A new grinder to fix a sour cup.** A sour cup from a burr grinder is a recipe problem, not a grinder problem — grind finer with the grinder already on the counter.
- **Darker beans to "cover" the sourness.** A darker roast hides the brightness instead of fixing the extraction, and adds its own bitter edge.
- **Bottles of distilled water for daily brewing.** Distilled water on its own brews a thin cup. Filtered tap does the job better.

## The question, answered

So why does the pour over keep coming out sour? Most likely under-extraction: the grind is too coarse, the water too cool, or the brew too short. Grind one step finer, taste, and only then move to hotter water or a longer brew. If the cup is sour and bitter at once, fix the pour before touching anything else. The beans are the last thing to change — and a sour cup is usually the recipe asking for more extraction, not a new bag or a new grinder.
