Home brewers ask the same question in every coffee forum: the water sits in the dripper, barely dripping, and by the time it finally finishes at five or six minutes the cup tastes bitter and harsh. On Home-Barista, a first-time poster filtering a cold-brew concentrate through a V60 watched the paper clog completely, and the replies named the cause at once: the micro-fine particles left after sieving pile into a tight layer that blocks the flow ([Home-Barista, May 29, 2020](https://www.home-barista.com/brewing/help-needed-coffee-filter-clogs-t65589.html)). The same clogging is what stalls an ordinary pour over — only the fines come from the grinder, not a sieve.

## What a stall is

A stall is when the water flow through the coffee bed and the filter slows dramatically or stops, usually near the end of the brew. Instead of a steady stream you get a slow trickle. Most single-cup pour-over recipes finish in roughly 2.5 to 4 minutes in total, including the bloom. If yours runs well past 4 or 5 minutes and tastes bitter, you are looking at a stall.

The extra contact time over-extracts the coffee, so the cup comes out bitter, harsh, and drying. The bed left behind is often dense and muddy, like wet clay instead of distinct particles. The usual villain is fines: dust-sized particles every grinder produces. As you pour, they migrate down and lodge in the pores of the paper, forming an almost watertight layer, much like a clogged drain. Blade grinders make far more fines than burr grinders, which crush beans between two burrs into a more even size. A quality burr grinder still makes some fines, just far fewer.

## Grind one or two steps coarser

A grind that is too fine is the most common cause, and the first thing to try. Finer grounds create more resistance to water flow and slow everything down — the coffee bed becomes a dam. Move one or two steps coarser first. A medium grind is the usual starting point for pour over. Adjust in small steps and taste each cup before moving again: if the coarser grind drains faster but the cup turns sour, the real problem is fines from the grinder or too much agitation, not the grind setting itself.

Look at the bed after the drawdown. If the grounds are muddy and flat like silt, the grind is too fine. If you can see individual particles and the bed has a slight cone shape, you are probably in the right range.

Two more grind fixes are worth knowing. Clean the burrs regularly: old grounds and oils built up in the grinder add to the problem. And sifting the grounds through a fine sieve removes fines and speeds up the drawdown — but it also wastes coffee and takes out particles that add body, so most brewers treat it as a quick test, not a routine.

## Pour gently and swirl less

Agitation helps extraction, but too much of it moves fines into the paper fast. A high, forceful stream churns the bed. Fast circles near the paper walls kick fines toward the filter. A hard swirl in the bloom or at the end can pack fines into the paper and choke the drawdown.

Pour gently with a gooseneck kettle, in slow, steady circles, keeping the stream inside the coffee and away from the filter wall. Try a recipe with fewer pours — each pour adds turbulence, so a five-pour recipe agitates more than a two-pour one. Swirl lightly or not at all: if the drawdown suddenly gets much longer after a swirl, you are packing fines. If you normally pour in several stages, try one slow, continuous pour after the bloom and compare the drawdown times.

## Check the filter and the mug

Paper type and thickness change the flow rate. Very thick papers, like Chemex filters, are slower by design, and cheap thin papers can clog or tear. Use the filter made for your brewer — Hario V60 02 filters for a V60 02, for example — and rinse it with hot water before brewing. Rinsing removes papery taste and helps the filter sit flush in the cone. A filter that is too big can fold or collapse into the cone, and one that is too small sits badly; either can block the outlet.

The mug can stall a brew too. Air has to escape from the cup below for coffee to drip in. If the dripper's base seals perfectly on the rim of the mug, trapped air pushes back and the flow slows. If a brew stalls, lift the dripper for a second to break the seal and see if the flow resumes. Brewing into a server with a different rim size avoids the problem altogether.

## Smaller batch, and the beans

A bigger dose means a deeper bed, which resists the water more and drains more slowly near the end. Pushing a large batch through a size 01 V60, or any standard-size dripper, is a common culprit. Brew a smaller dose, or move up to a larger dripper and matching filters for bigger batches.

The beans themselves can be the cause. Light roasts are harder and more brittle, so they tend to shatter into more fines, and different coffees can drain at different speeds at the same setting. If you only stall with one bag, the coffee is probably the cause: grind that coffee a little coarser and agitate less than usual, and expect to adjust the grind for each new coffee.

## What not to buy

- **A new grinder to fix one stall.** A stall is usually a grind-setting or pouring problem, not a grinder problem — go coarser and pour more gently before buying anything.
- **A different dripper before fixing the grind and the pour.** Drippers are built for different flow rates, and a slower dripper usually needs a coarser grind than a fast one. Changing the dripper without changing the recipe just moves the stall.
- **A big bag of a bean you have not tried.** A new bag can stall at the same setting as the last one. Buy the size you finish while it is fresh, and change one thing at a time so the fix is visible.

## The question, answered

So why does the pour over take so long? Most likely the grind is too fine, or fines from the grinder have clogged the paper — and hard pouring or swirling pushed them there. Grind one or two steps coarser, pour gently in slow circles, use the filter made for the dripper, and check that the dripper is not sealing airtight on the mug. A single-cup pour over should finish in about 2.5 to 4 minutes. If the cup still stalls after that, look at the beans: light roasts shatter into more fines, and a deep bed from a big batch drains slowly near the end.
